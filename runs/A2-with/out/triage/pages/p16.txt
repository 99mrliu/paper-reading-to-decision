VII. CONCLUSION
We have given a per-query accounting of hybrid retrieval
that treats “do not fuse” as a first-class outcome. With a
standard Anserini BM25, dense encoders from 22M to 596M
parameters, and a learned-sparse alternative, the best fusion
method beats the deployable dense-only retriever in aggre-
gate on every corpus with the primary encoder and on 33 of
the 35 encoder–corpus pairs, yet demotes relevant documents
below the better component on 13–48% of queries and below
dense-only on 8–21%, with losses on a hurt query of the same
order as the gains on a helped one and significant downside
risk under loss-averse evaluation on six of the seven corpora.
The tax is mostly a ranking effect (recall@10 is preserved
in aggregate, although a minority of the hurt queries lose
a relevant document from the top-10), it is most likely on
the queries that only one retriever covers (though on corpora
where both retrievers usually succeed, most hurt queries are
ones both cover), and it persists across the encoder ladder
and the SPLADE substitution; the weight that maximizes
aggregate nDCG@10 transfers across corpora under held-out
selection, while cheap per-query routing does not, because
the features that predict failure change meaning with the
sparse–dense balance. A cross-encoder second stage removes
part of the tax only where it is itself reliable, overwrites it
where it is not, and leaves interpolated and dense candidate
sets within 0.011 nDCG@10 of each other once reranked
with the one reranker and depth tested, except where sparse
and dense are balanced. The practical guidance follows the
mechanism: measure the sparse–dense balance on the target
corpus, choose dense-weighted interpolation where dense
leads and rank fusion where the two are even, keep dense-
only on the table for rank-sensitive consumers, and re-check
the decision when the corpus or the encoder changes.
