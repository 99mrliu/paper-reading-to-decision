ABSTRACT Hybrid retrieval, which fuses a sparse lexical retriever with a dense neural one, is widely
recommended in the software documentation surveyed in September 2026, usually with reciprocal rank
fusion (RRF), on the strength of aggregate gains. We ask what fusion costs on individual queries, treating
“do not fuse” as a first-class outcome, in the setting in which hybrid retrieval is deployed: a standard Anserini
BM25, five dense encoders spanning 22M–596M parameters and three model generations, a learned-sparse
(SPLADE) alternative, and a cross-encoder second stage, on seven BEIR datasets and three fusion functions.
Even the best method, dense-weighted score interpolation, ranks relevant documents below the better of its
two components on 13–48% of queries and below the deployable dense-only retriever on 8–21% (14–
48% and 11–25% at a weight selected under leave-one-corpus-out validation), while beating dense-only
in aggregate on every corpus with the primary encoder; the downside is significant under loss-averse risk-
sensitive evaluation on six of the seven corpora. We call this loss the fusion tax. It is mostly a ranking
effect that preserves recall in aggregate, it is most likely on the queries that only one retriever covers, and
it persists across the encoder ladder and the SPLADE substitution. RRF carries the higher tax wherever
the dense retriever is clearly stronger and only beats dense-only where the two components are balanced.
Fusion failure is weakly predictable within a corpus but transfers unreliably across corpora, because the
features that predict it change sign with the sparse–dense balance. A cross-encoder recovers part of the tax
only where it is itself reliable. We distill these findings into guidance that keys the fusion decision to the
measured sparse–dense balance of the target corpus.

I. INTRODUCTION
Hybrid retrieval, which fuses a sparse lexical retriever such
as BM25 [1] with a dense neural retriever [2], is, at the
time of writing, the default recommendation of most cur-
rent search stacks and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
frameworks, and reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) [3] is the
fusion method most of them recommend or ship as the only
option (Section II-B). The recommendation rests on aggre-
gate evidence: averaged over a query set, the fused ranking
usually beats either component.
That fusion can help on average while hurting individual
queries is not a new observation. Risk-sensitive evaluation of
rank fusion [4], fusion-oriented query performance predic-
tion [5], [6], and per-query routing between retrievers [7]–
[9] all start from it. What those lines do not provide is
the quantity a practitioner deciding between a hybrid and a
single strong retriever needs: how often, and by how much, a
modern sparse–dense fusion ranks relevant documents below
the better of its own two components, and below the single
component one would deploy instead. We call this per-query
loss the fusion tax, we treat “do not fuse” as a first-class
outcome, and we measure the tax in the setting in which
hybrid retrieval is actually deployed today: a standard BM25
pipeline, dense encoders spanning 22M–596M parameters
and three model generations, a learned-sparse alternative, and
a cross-encoder second stage.
