C. SCORE CALIBRATION VERSUS RANK FUSION
When is score fusion preferable to rank fusion? The
metasearch literature answered this early: Lee [18] attributed
fusion gains to the “unequal overlap” of relevant versus
non-relevant documents across runs, Vogt and Cottrell [19]
characterized when a linear combination of scores helps, and
Renda and Straccia [38] found score-based aggregation supe-
rior to rank-based aggregation whenever scores are available
and can be normalized. The normalization step is the crux:
simple min–max or z-score normalization [39] is sensitive to
outliers and to the depth of the list, whereas score-distribution
models [40], [41] calibrate scores on a relevance scale at
the cost of fitting a model per run, and the interpolation
weight itself can be set from component effectiveness [42].
For sparse–dense hybrids, Ma et al. [43] and Chen et al. [44]
used weighted score interpolation and reported consistent
zero-shot gains, and Bruch et al. [45] showed that a con-
vex combination of min–max normalized scores outperforms
RRF and is sample-efficient to tune. The practical reading
is that interpolation wins when the two score distributions
can be put on a comparable scale and one component is
reliably stronger, because it keeps the stronger component’s
confidence; rank fusion is the safer choice when scores are
incomparable or unavailable, or when the two components
are similarly strong, so that neither should dominate. Our
results reproduce both halves of this picture (Section V-B).
D. QUERY PERFORMANCE PREDICTION
Query Performance Prediction (QPP) estimates retrieval
quality without relevance judgments [46]. Post-retrieval pre-
dictors are computed from the score distribution of a single
ranking: NQC [46] measures the standard deviation of top
scores and WIG [47] the gain of the top results over the
collection mean; both are free, since the retrieval scores are
already in hand. Faggioli et al. [48] report that classical
predictors degrade on neural rankers, which is one reason
cheap routing over dense retrievers is hard. Recent work
applies QPP to weight the components in fusion [49]. We
ask instead whether the same signals predict fusion failure,
and whether that prediction transfers across corpora.
E. SELECTIVE RETRIEVAL AND PER-QUERY ROUTING
Routing studies differ in what the per-query decision is.
Some decide whether to retrieve at all [50]; some choose
which retriever or pipeline to run, from sparse versus dense
on efficiency grounds [7] to a mixture of expert embedding
models [51] or a complexity-tiered pipeline [52], [53]; and a

growing group sets the fusion weight per query, from an LLM
judge of each component’s likely effectiveness (DAT [9]) to
query-specificity heuristics [33], zero-shot mixtures of sparse
and dense retrievers [54], margin-based confidence [55], or a
learned regressor from the query representation [56]. Mothe
and Ullah [8] frame configuration selection as a risk-sensitive
decision. All of these presume that per-query component
effectiveness is reliably learnable. Our work is complemen-
tary and diagnostic: rather than proposing another router, we
characterize when fusion fails and test whether the cheap
signals such routers rely on transfer across corpora, finding
that they do not (Section V-G). This also suggests why DAT
resorts to an LLM judge: the signal it needs is not available
from cheap, transferable query features.
F. RISK-SENSITIVE FUSION AND FUSION-QPP
That fusion can harm individual queries while helping on
average has roots in risk-sensitive evaluation [57], [58]. Ben-
ham and Culpepper [4] gave a per-query risk–reward view
of rank fusion, reporting that unsupervised fusion improves
most topics but degrades a minority (e.g. 142 improved vs.
21 worsened on Robust04). The fusion-QPP line [5], [6]
predicts the absolute effectiveness of an already-fused list
from reference lists; because it never compares fusion to a
component, it cannot establish that fusion hurts, and because
its estimators are fit per collection, it makes no cross-corpus
claim. Table 1 positions our study against these lines. The
fusion tax descends from the risk framing but differs in the
reference: wins and losses in [4] are scored against a single
weak BM25 run with a ±10% deadband, so a query can be a
win there (fusion beats weak BM25) yet a loss for us (fusion
below dense-only). We score loss against the better com-
ponent per query and against the deployable always-dense
system, in a modern sparse–dense setting, across an encoder
scale ladder and a learned-sparse substitution, under held-out
weight selection, and we test whether the loss is predictable
across corpora and recoverable by reranking. Each prior line
covers at most a few of these axes, and none measures the
loss against the better component or against the deployable
single retriever; two concurrent aggregate-level studies [59],
[60] corroborate the phenomenon without measuring it per
query.
