B. FUSION METHOD COMPARISON AND THE
INTERPOLATION WEIGHT
Table 5 reports nDCG@10 for every method with BGE-
small. The aggregate picture replicates Bruch et al. [45]
where their conditions hold and departs from it where they
do not. Dense-weighted interpolation (α = 0.7) is the best
fusion method on five of the seven corpora, all of them
corpora on which dense retrieval leads BM25 clearly, by
0.04–0.18 nDCG@10 (0.039 on SciDocs): SciFact, FiQA,
ArguAna, SciDocs, and Quora; its advantage over RRF is
significant on four of the five after Bonferroni correction
(Supplementary Table S4: FiQA, ArguAna, SciDocs, Quora,
p < 0.001; SciFact p = 0.07). On the two corpora where
the two components are balanced, the picture reverses: on
NFCorpus (BM25 0.325 vs. dense 0.337) the three fusion
methods are within 0.003 of one another, and on TREC-
COVID (0.656 vs. 0.666) rank fusion and CombMNZ (0.801
and 0.805) beat dense-heavy interpolation (0.717) and dense-
only (0.666) by a wide and significant margin (p = 0.002).
This is the classical prediction of the calibration literature
(Section II-C): score interpolation wins when one component
is reliably stronger and its confidence should be kept, rank
fusion when neither should dominate. RRF is also frequently
no better than the dense retriever alone: it trails dense-only on
five corpora (significantly on ArguAna and Quora) and beats
it only on the two balanced ones.
The interpolation optimum is interior and transfers
across corpora. Two questions arise: whether the optimum
is really interior rather than dense-only, and whether a weight
chosen on one set of queries survives on another. Sweeping
α from 0.5 to 1.0 (Supplementary Table S7) answers the first:
the in-sample optimum is 0.7 on four corpora, 0.6 on NFCor-
pus, 0.8 on ArguAna, and 0.5 on TREC-COVID, never 1.0,
and dense-only is strictly worse than the best interpolation on
every corpus (e.g. FiQA 0.409 vs. 0.385, ArguAna 0.605 vs.
0.595, TREC-COVID 0.748 vs. 0.666); per query the optimal
weight is rarely unique, because nDCG@10 is discrete: on
23–58% of queries every weight on the 0.1 grid scores
the same (2% on TREC-COVID), dense-only (α = 1) is
among the optimal weights on 52–84% of queries on six
corpora (18% on TREC-COVID), some fused weight strictly
beats dense-only on 16–48% (82% on TREC-COVID), a
strictly interior weight is required on 8–30% (70% on TREC-
COVID), and a near-dense weight (α ≥0.9) is strictly
required on only 6–16%. Table 4 answers the second with
two held-out protocols. Under leave-one-corpus-out (LOCO)
selection, the weight chosen on the other six corpora is 0.6 or
0.7 for every held-out corpus, and applying it costs at most
0.014 nDCG@10 relative to the in-sample optimum on six
corpora (the largest, ArguAna, 0.605 vs. 0.591) and 0.031
on TREC-COVID, the one corpus whose in-sample optimum
(α = 0.5) lies outside the transferred range; under within-
corpus 5-fold cross-validation the selected weight coincides
with the in-sample optimum on every corpus (SciFact’s folds
vary between 0.5 and 0.9, with mode 0.7) and the held-out
nDCG@10 is within 0.010 of it. At the held-out weights the
deployable tax is 10.6–25.0% (LOCO) and 8.2–26.0% (CV),
against 8.2–20.9% at the fixed α = 0.7: on five corpora the
transferred weight, being slightly less dense-heavy than the
in-sample optimum, raises it by 2–6 points, and the oracle
tax at the held-out weights is 14–48% (LOCO) and 13–
46% (CV). A dense-heavy weight of 0.6–0.7 is therefore a
defensible transferable default, and the tax reported below
is not an artifact of selecting the weight on the evaluation
queries; if anything, a weight chosen elsewhere pays slightly
more. The one corpus that prefers a balanced weight, TREC-
COVID, illustrates the trade-off: moving to α = 0.5 raises
its mean nDCG@10 from 0.717 to 0.748 while raising the
fraction of queries that fall below dense-only from 14% to
26%.
