F. ROBUSTNESS TO THE SPARSE METHOD: SPLADE
The ladder rules out a weak dense model; we now rule out a
weak sparse one by replacing BM25 with SPLADE [16] as
the sparse side of fusion with BGE-small on all seven corpora
(Table 10). Pairing a learned-sparse retriever with a dense
one is not new (Bruch et al. include SPLADE among their
components); the contribution is the per-component attribu-
tion of the tax. SPLADE is a stronger sparse retriever than
BM25 on six corpora (e.g. 0.708 vs. 0.665 on SciFact, 0.356
vs. 0.236 on FiQA, 0.725 vs. 0.656 on TREC-COVID) and
ties it on SciDocs, and SPLADE+dense interpolation beats
dense-only in aggregate on six corpora and ties it on Quora.
Yet interpolation’s tax does not abate: SPLADE+dense inter-
polation hurts 14–41% of queries on six corpora and 60% on
TREC-COVID, comparable to or higher than BM25+dense
on five of the seven and lower by under three points on the
other two (ArguAna, SciDocs). Interpolation’s tax is thus
intrinsic to score fusion, robust to strengthening either side.
For RRF the picture is different in kind. With the standard
Anserini BM25 the sparse upgrade moves RRF’s tax by only
−6.2 to +7.1 points, lowering it on four corpora (most on
ArguAna, 33.9% vs. 40.1%) and raising it on three. The
large RRF reductions that upgrading a weak lexical baseline
produces are visible in the BM25 swap itself: moving from
rank_bm25 to Anserini lowered RRF’s tax from 25–48%
to 20–40% and raised its nDCG@10 on every corpus while
changing interpolation’s tax by at most 5.3 points (Supple-
mentary Table S1). RRF’s tax therefore responds to the
quality of the lexical component in a way interpolation’s
does not, which is consistent with rank fusion’s inability to
discount a weak component’s confident-looking ranks; once
the lexical side is standard, however, a substantial RRF tax
remains (19–46% with SPLADE).
